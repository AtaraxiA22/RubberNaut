Dear Marla,

The bus shuddered with the weight of humanity, crammed into a space that felt impossibly small. I sank deeper into my seat, clutching my duffel bag-a lifeline stuffed with clothes, each fold hiding the shame of who I was beneath my weary exterior. As I glanced out the smeared window, the world outside blurred, each building and street sign a reminder of the reality I now fled from.

A year ago I owned an almost-new Mustang convertible and lived in an upscale neighborhood of Thorpton. Last night I slept in a shelter for the homeless. Now, on the bus, I was heading toward whatever future I could find. Perhaps a job.

The exhaust fumes mixed with the scent of wet pavement and unwashed bodies, swirling through the air like a grim reminder of my situation. Around me, faces slumped in the muted haze of morning, yet I felt like a beacon of vibrant emotion amid the mundane-a walk in a monochrome landscape. I noticed the man in front of me, a paint-splattered figure lost in his own world, and the mother whose toddler tugged on her coat, both oblivious to the burden of life carried by the rest of us.

Beneath my jeans, the latex underwear clung to my skin like a confounding whisper of my hidden desires. Just this morning, I'd made an impulsive choice, slipping them on for a flicker of comfort amidst the storm of despair Frank had left in his wake. The thought of his voice-sharp and unyielding-replayed in my mind like a haunting refrain: "Fucking pervert." My heart clenched at the reminder, thrumming with shame, while my fingers tightened around the bag, as if it could shield me from his rejection.

My reflection flickered by in the window: silver hair gathered in a frayed ponytail, weary eyes tracing the edges of crow's feet deepened by sleepless nights. I looked every bit the part of a woman weathered by life's storms, burdened but unyielding, encased in a practical jacket that hid my secret. But was it really a secret?

As the bus lumbered through cracked asphalt, I caught glimpses of city life-a vibrant tapestry of graffiti, burnt-out buildings, and hints of neglected beauty. I felt a pang of longing for the warmth of a life that felt unreachable. Each stop passed was like a crack in the foundation of my existence, reminding me of how fragile my position had become, how everything felt unmoored.

Then, as the diesel engine groaned to a halt, I spotted it-a gleaming sanctuary of possibility: the public library. My heart surged at the sight, a quiet fortress amidst the chaos. I grabbed my bag and, with a racing pulse, pressed the stop button. The doors hissed open, welcoming cool air that washed over me like breath itself.

Stepping onto the sidewalk, a mingling of anxiety and hope roiled in my gut. My fingers trembled around the strap of my bag as I walked briskly toward the stone entrance. Each step was an act of defiance, a rejection of the chaos behind me. Inside, the quiet hush was a balm against my frayed nerves, the air thick with the smell of paper and the ghosts of countless stories, inviting me in.

With purpose, I crossed the threshold, each heartbeat echoing in the stillness, reminding me that this moment held the promise of something new-a chance to begin again.

The library smelled the same way it had when I was younger-paper, dust, and the faint mustiness of climate control straining against the Pacific Northwest's relentless damp. I stood just inside the entrance, my duffel bag heavy on my shoulder, and felt the weight of it like an accusation. This was the third library in as many weeks interrupted by the holiday season, and I knew how this would end. Still, I'd come anyway, because the alternative was spending the last of my cash on another night in a motel that smelled like cigarettes and broken promises.

The mid-century building had charm once. High ceilings. Blonde wood trim. Wide windows that let in pale January light. Some architect had cared about this place when it was built, had believed people would come here and find something. Now it felt like a stage set nobody needed anymore. The teenagers were on laptops. The elderly patrons occupied the worn armchairs near the reading section. A mother with a child who wouldn't stop fidgeting sat near the children's corner. Nobody looked up as I made my way to the front desk.

![My first stop was of necessity - the bathroom. As I washed my hands the latex called to me. Yup. I saw it in the mirror.]({{ '/resources/Rn+2016.0104-0900.jpg' | relative_url }})

The librarian was in her sixties, maybe seventies, with short iron-gray hair and glasses on a beaded chain. She had the efficient, slightly weary expression of someone who'd already made fifty decisions before nine in the morning. I waited while she checked in a stack of returns, keeping my duffel close. The latex underwear clung to my skin-I'd put it on this morning in the shelter's bathroom, a small rebellion, a private comfort in the shape of a material that clung to me like a second thought I couldn't shake. Nobody could see it. Nobody could know. It was the only thing I still had that was entirely mine.

"Can I help you?" the librarian asked, glancing up from her computer.

"I'm looking for work," I said. My voice came out dry. "I saw on your board last week that you sometimes had job postings from local businesses."

She nodded without warmth. "We do have a community board. Mostly retail and service positions. Are you looking for something specific?"

"Anything," I said. "I'm-I'm flexible. I've done housekeeping, retail, food service."

"We don't have anything here at the library itself, I'm afraid. Positions are posted by external employers. You're welcome to check the board by the entrance."

The "I'm afraid" was the worst part. It wasn't mean. It was polite. Which made it final. She'd already moved on to the next patron, a man in a wool coat asking about computer access. I stepped back, my shoulders already beginning their familiar collapse inward.

I'd been a dancer once. I took ballet lessons all through high school. Nothing professional-community theater, a few freelance gigs doing choreography for small productions-but I'd carried myself differently then. I was in the Spring of my life then. Now I was nearly a half-century into a different woman's life-I had a Medicare card in my wallet now-one who'd made choices that Frank had decided were disqualifying. The moment he'd found my collection in the closet-the rubber suit, the latex hoods, the rest-something had simply ended. Not the relationship. The end came after. This was the aftermath, the long, slow compression of possibility into nothing.

I'd been living with him for almost a decade. The Mustang was his idea. The house in the nice neighborhood was his idea. The whole vanilla life was his idea, and I'd gone along because I was good at going along. I was good at pretending. But then he'd gone through my things-looking for something else, some bill he'd misplaced-and there it was. The rubber. My secret. My skin.

"Fucking pervert," he'd said, holding the suit at arm's length like it was contaminated. His voice had been quiet, which was worse than shouting. "Get out of my house."

I hadn't expected him to mean it. But he'd had my things packed and waiting by the front door the next morning. No conversation. No room for explanation. Just: this is what you are, and I don't want it in my home.

Now I was a woman with a duffel bag full of clothes and a latex catsuit I'd owned for fifteen years. I had three hundred and forty-two dollars in my checking account. I'd been bouncing between shelters and cheap motels for five weeks.

The bulletin board was near the entrance, where I'd seen it on my previous visits. It was crowded with the usual debris of small-town desperation: handwritten notices for roommates, used furniture, gigs for musicians, tutoring services. Most of the job postings were from fast-food places or retail chains. I'd already called those. They all wanted to hire people with references they could check, with recent employment history they could verify. I had a gap that would require explaining, and I wasn't good at explaining anymore.

My fingers moved down the board mechanically, scanning titles and phone numbers. I was aware of other patrons around me-their productivity, their purpose. A woman with a laptop working on something that looked like spreadsheets. A man reading a printed article about solar panels. Everyone here seemed to know where they were going. Everyone seemed to fit.

That's when I saw it.

The card was handwritten on index stock, dog-eared at the corners and slightly yellowed. The ink was smudged, like whoever had written it had been using a ballpoint pen that was running low. But the words were clear enough:

**WANTED: DOMESTIC ASSISTANT / HOUSEKEEPER**

Light Duty. Room & Board + Stipend.

Live-in required. VERY ISOLATED LOCATION.

No Visitors. Start Immediately.

call 123-555-1212

I read it three times.

Isolation. That word sat in my chest like a stone dropped into still water. I'd spent the last five weeks surrounded by people I didn't know-shelter residents and motel clerks and the hollow kindness of social workers who meant well but saw me as one more problem to be checked off. The idea of isolation sounded less like a threat and more like mercy.

Room and board meant I wouldn't be sleeping in a shelter or under a bridge. It meant eating something other than convenience store sandwiches. It meant a door that locked and a place to keep my things-including the things nobody else needed to know about.

Light duty meant I could do it. Whatever it was, I could do it.

The card had been on the board for what looked like a while. The corners were soft. That was either a very good sign or a very bad one. Maybe they were desperate enough not to ask too many questions. Maybe they were the kind of people who didn't care about your background or your references or the ways you'd failed to fit into the lives people had built for you.

Or maybe it was exactly as it sounded: a job in the middle of nowhere with people who wanted to be left alone. I understood that impulse. I'd wanted to be left alone my entire life. I just hadn't known how.

I pulled out a small notebook from the side pocket of my duffel. The pencil I found was stubby, barely two inches long, worn down by my own hand over the past few weeks. I wrote down the number in careful letters, my handwriting betraying the tremor in my fingers:

555-1212

Domestic Assistant. Room & Board. Isolated.

I looked at the clock on the wall above the reference desk. It was 10:47 in the morning. The library would close at 5:30. I had plenty of time before I'd have to leave, before I'd have to navigate back to the streets and figure out where to sleep tonight.

I copied the number one more time in the margin of the notebook, just to be sure I had it right. My hand was shaking. When had my hands started shaking constantly? Sometime in week three of homelessness, I thought. Or maybe it was grief. Maybe it was just the cold.

The latex underwear shifted slightly against my skin as I shifted my weight. The small compression, the whisper of the material-it was all the privacy I had left. All the proof that there was a version of me nobody else got to judge or reject or destroy. Under the clothes, under the poverty, under the homelessness, I was still Hevea's. I was still the woman who needed rubber the way other people needed air.

I looked at the card one more time, then back at the number in my notebook.

Very isolated location. No visitors.

It sounded like a monastery. It sounded like a place where someone could disappear into the person they actually were, instead of performing the person everyone else expected them to be. It sounded like a place where rubber might not be something to hide.

Or it sounded like exactly the kind of situation where nobody would find out about you until it was too late.

I tucked the notebook back into my duffel and looked around the library. The mother and child had moved to a different section. The man with the solar panel article was making notes. The woman with the spreadsheet was still working. None of them looked up. None of them knew that I was standing here with my entire future folded into a phone number on a piece of paper.

I had an hour before I needed to decide whether to call.

I found a chair in a quiet corner near the reference section and sat down to wait. The weight of the duffel beside me felt slightly different now-less like an accusation and more like a suitcase full of possibilities.

Very isolated. Start immediately. Room and board.

I repeated it to myself like a mantra, the way I used to do with the smell of latex, with the feel of compression against my skin. Like a prayer. Like a lifeline.

I found a pay phone in the library's back hallway, near the restrooms, out of sight of the main reading area. It was the kind of thing that still existed in places like this-old, battered by decades of use, the kind of phone that made me feel like I was calling from a different era. I fed my coins into the slot and listened to them drop with satisfying clicks. The dial tone was steady. Real. I held the receiver against my ear and tried to remember how to breathe normally.

![My fingers trembled as I dialed. It was stupid to be this nervous. It was just a job inquiry. People called about job inquiries all the time. But people with homes called about jobs. People with credit histories and references and recent employment. I was calling from a pay phone in a library while wearing homeless clothes and latex underwear, and I was about to beg for a position that asked for nothing but isolation and availability.]({{ '/resources/Rn+2016.0104-0901.jpg' | relative_url }})

The phone rang four times before someone answered.

"Yes?" A man's voice. Calm. Not exactly warm, but not hostile either. The kind of voice that suggested he was used to being listened to.

"Hi," I said. My voice came out higher than I wanted it to. I cleared my throat. "I'm calling about the domestic assistant position?"

A pause. Not uncomfortable, just a measured silence that suggested he was looking for the card in his mind, or deciding whether this was worth his time.

"Yes," he said finally. "How did you find that posting?"

"At the Thorpton library. On the community board by the entrance."

"Ah. Good. That's actually how we wanted it advertised. I'm James Hahn. I placed that notice." He said it like it was important that I know he was the one responsible for it. "Can I ask your name?"

"Thalia," I said. Then: "Thalia Koster."

"Thalia," he repeated, as if testing the word. "Have you worked in housekeeping before?"

"Yes," I said. "I've done light cleaning, laundry, meal preparation. I'm reliable. I work well independently."

This was technically true. I'd done housekeeping for husbands and live-in boyfriends for decades before I met Frank. I'd cleaned houses for a residential service company. The job had been fine-low pressure, physical work that didn't require conversation. I'd been good at it. But I'd stopped because Frank had said it was beneath me, that he could support us both, that I should find something more interesting. That meant staying at home, maintaining and enclosed by it.

Now I would have killed for that job back.

"The position involves maintaining a house for myself and my wife," James said. His tone was matter-of-fact, businesslike. "We're both retired. We don't require much-basic cleaning, laundry, meal prep as you mentioned. The work is light. That's not negotiable-my wife is in a wheelchair, and we've designed our home and routines around minimizing physical demand on both of us. You'd need to give her assistance occasionally."

I felt something shift in my chest. A wheelchair. A wife. A man who was organized and thoughtful enough to think about physical demand. This didn't sound like a trap. This sounded like a real household with real needs.

"I can do that," I said.

"There are some important things you need to understand about the position," James continued. His voice had taken on a slightly sharper edge-not harsh, but more deliberate. "Our property is extremely isolated. We're located approximately one hour from the nearest grocery store, accessible only by logging roads that are often in poor condition. There is no cellular service. Internet is via satellite and is unreliable. We generate our own electricity."

I pressed my free hand against my chest, feeling the latex compress slightly beneath my shirt. The physical pressure was grounding. Real. It steadied me.

"No visitors are permitted," James said. The words came out evenly, like a fact of physics rather than a rule. "This is non-negotiable. We value our privacy above all else. This is not a social environment. You would be responsible for housekeeping duties. Outside of meals and necessary household interactions, we maintain separate schedules and spaces."

I thought about the shelter. About the motel rooms with paper-thin walls. About the woman two beds over in the shelter who'd had a cough that never stopped, and the constant low-level anxiety of shared spaces and people who were themselves desperate and therefore unpredictable.

No visitors. Separate spaces. Privacy.

It sounded like heaven.

"I understand," I said. "I'm comfortable with that. I actually prefer solitude."

"You do?" James said. There was something in his tone that suggested he was assessing this answer, turning it over. "Tell me why."

I paused. This was the kind of question that could unmask you if you answered wrong. I thought about Frank. About the shelter. About years of trying to be acceptable in shared spaces.

"I'm private by nature," I said carefully. "I've spent the past few weeks in transitional housing, and I've found that I don't do well with a lot of social interaction. I focus better when I have my own space. I'm more productive. The isolation doesn't bother me-it actually appeals to me."

Another pause. I could almost hear him thinking.

"What about your recent employment history?" James asked. "The notice says start immediately, which means I need someone who's available now. Are you currently employed?"

This was the moment where a lie could help me. This was the moment where I could say I'd just quit a job, or was between positions, or anything other than the truth. But something about his voice-the way he'd been specific about needing someone available-suggested that he wouldn't care about the conventional narrative. That maybe someone in his world understood the concept of people falling through the cracks.

"I'm between situations," I said. "I'm available immediately. Today, if necessary."

"Where are you located now?" James asked.

"At the Thorpton library on 14th Street. I came here to use their job search resources."

"Ah," James said. "That's actually convenient. How would you feel about coming here for an in-person interview? I could meet you at the library in an hour or so."

One hour. He'd be here in one hour. I was being offered an actual interview by an actual person for an actual job that came with shelter and food and isolation.

"Yes," I said. The word came out in a rush. I tried to steady myself. "Yes, that works. What time?"

"Shall we say twelve noon?" James said. "I'll look for you in the library. I'll be driving an ancient forest-green Toyota truck. I'll be wearing a blue jacket. I have silver hair. Does that work for you?"

"Yes," I said again. "Twelve noon. I'll meet you there."

"Excellent," James said. "What should I be looking for? What are you wearing?

I glanced down at myself. "Gray jacket. White shirt underneath. Jeans. I have a duffel bag with me."

"Right. Twelve noon, then. I look forward to meeting you, Thalia."

He hung up before I could respond.

I stood in the hallway with the dead receiver still pressed to my ear, trying to process what had just happened. The dial tone buzzed like a small insect. I hung up slowly, my hand unsteady.

One hour. Sixty minutes. Three thousand six hundred seconds until either my life changed or I was back on the streets with an additional rejection to carry.

I walked back to the main reading area and found my original chair. The duffel bag was still there, unmoved. I sat down and pulled out my notebook, trying to write down the details James had given me-the description of his truck, his appearance, the time. But my handwriting was shaking too badly to make the words legible.

I pressed my hand against my chest again, feeling the latex beneath my clothes. It was still there. Still real. Still mine. No matter what happened in the next hour, that part of me was untouched. That part of me was safe.

I had one hour to decide if I was brave enough to disappear into isolation. One hour to decide if a man with a measured voice and specific rules was trustworthy. One hour to decide if isolation was actually mercy or if it was a different kind of prison.

The clock above the reference desk read 10:58.

I had time.

I watched the clock edge toward noon. The second hand was conspicuously loud, each tick a small hammer against my resolve. I'd positioned myself in a chair that faced the library's main entrance, which was both practical and torturous. Every time the door opened, my heartbeat spiked. A woman in a rain jacket. A teenager with headphones. A man whose only purpose was apparently to check the public bathroom sign. None of them were James Hahn.

I'd gone to the bathroom twice. Once to splash water on my face and make sure I didn't have anything in my teeth. Once just to move around, to burn off the nervous energy that was making my leg bounce against the floor in a rhythm I couldn't control. The latex underwear was still there, still pressing against my skin, and I'd pulled my gray jacket tighter around myself, as if that would help hide what I was or, more accurately, as if the rubber might give me courage through pure proximity.

By 11:55, I was watching the door with the intensity of someone defusing a bomb. I'd convinced myself he wouldn't come. That the whole thing had been a test, or a prank, or that he'd decided between the time I hung up and now that I wasn't suitable. People were constantly deciding I wasn't suitable. My previous husbands. Frank. The shelter worker who'd looked at my application and asked if I had anyone who could vouch for my stability.

At 11:59, the door opened. Green Toyota truck. Blue jacket. Silver hair in a ponytail.

James Hahn was standing in the library entrance like a man who'd decided to step into a foreign country and was already cataloging the differences. His eyes moved methodically across the space-the circulation desk, the reading areas, the reference section-before landing on me. He was exactly as he'd described himself but also entirely different from what I'd imagined. Older, for one thing. Early seventies at least, with the weathered look of someone who spent time outdoors. His face was lined in ways that suggested both thought and exposure-sun damage mixed with the kind of creases that come from frowning in concentration.

He was wearing a Moody Blues t-shirt under the blue jacket. The shirt was vintage, faded, clearly beloved. The print showed their logo from some long-ago concert tour. He'd chosen it deliberately, I thought. He was the kind of person who chose his shirts. He had a ponytail.

He moved across the library floor with a deliberate pace that wasn't quite slow but wasn't rushed either. There was something meditative about his movement, like he'd thought about each step before taking it. He walked the way I danced-economical, aware of the body in space. He walked the way someone walked who'd been trained by something that had nothing to do with being fashionable.

His eyes found me a second time and held.

I stood up before he reached me, which was probably too eager, but I couldn't stay seated while he was walking toward me. My duffel bag sat at my feet like a small monument to my circumstances.

"Thalia?" he said. His voice in person was the same as on the phone-measured, calm, with an underlying texture of authority that wasn't performative. It was just how he was. "I'm James Hahn."

"Yes," I said. "Thank you for coming."

We shook hands. His grip was firm but not aggressive, the hand of someone who did physical work and knew the value of a genuine handshake. His skin was warm, scarred on the back from something-a burn, maybe, or old injury work. "Shall we sit?" he asked, gesturing toward a quieter corner near the reference section.

I grabbed my duffel bag and followed him. We sat in two upholstered chairs that faced each other at an angle, the kind of arrangement people used when they wanted to talk but not be directly confrontational.

"Before I tell you more about the position," James said, "I'd like to understand something about you. You said on the phone that you're between situations. I need you to be direct about what that means. I'm not asking to judge you. I'm asking because it affects how I understand your reliability."

I'd prepared for this question but not its directness. Most people danced around it. They asked about your employment history or your references or they did a background check and didn't mention what they found. James just asked.

"I was living with someone," I said carefully. "That situation ended. I've been in transitional housing for the past few weeks. I'm available immediately because I'm already displaced."

James nodded slowly, like he was filing this information into a larger context. "Did you leave by choice or by circumstance?"

"By circumstance," I said. There was no point in lying. "But I'm stable enough to work. I’ve been a housewife most of my life. I have a lifetime of experience in managing homes and meeting the needs of those who lived in them. I'm pretty good at it."

"Why did you leave your previous housekeeping job?" he asked. "You mentioned you'd done it before."

"My circumstances changed," I said. "I was in a relationship where my partner preferred that I not work. That relationship ended, so I'm looking for work again."

It was technically true. It just omitted the fact that I'd been evicted, homeless, and wearing latex underwear in a shelter. Some truths were better presented in fragments.

James was watching me with the kind of attention that suggested he was reading more than what I was saying. It was disconcerting. Most people didn't pay that much attention. Most people were satisfied with the surface level of things. But James seemed to be looking at the edges, the places where my story didn't quite fit together in a conventional way.

"I'll be honest with you," he said. "We've had three people apply for this position in the past six months. All three lasted less than two weeks. They said the isolation was too much. They said they felt trapped. One of them claimed she'd had no idea how remote it was, despite the word 'isolated' being in the posting."

"I'm not going to have that problem," I said.

"How can you be certain?"

I thought about the shelter. The constant background noise of other people's crises. The woman next to me coughing. The man two beds over crying about custody hearings. The social worker's voice asking me if I'd thought about contacting family. The motel rooms where I could hear every footstep in the hall, every conversation through the walls. The feeling of being constantly observed, constantly evaluated.

"Because I've spent my whole life trying to fit into social situations," I said. "And I'm not very good at it. Isolation isn't a punishment for me. It's a relief. I thrive in it."

Something flickered across James's face at my last sentence. Recognition, maybe. Or understanding. "Tell me about your current living situation."

"I'm staying at different shelters," I said. "Thorpton has a rotation of facilities. I've been cycling through them. It's adequate, but it's not sustainable long-term."

"Are you safe?" he asked. The question was straightforward, without judgment.

"Yes," I said. "The facilities are safe. But they're not private, and they're temporary, and I'm trying to find something more stable." The fact that he asked that question gave me a clue of what sort of person he was.

James leaned back in his chair. "My wife's name is Lorraine. She's in a wheelchair. She has been since a car accident about eight years ago. We've adapted our life and our property to accommodate that. Our house is set up so that Lorraine can move independently within it. The housekeeping position is designed to help with what she can't physically manage-laundry, heavy cleaning, meal prep. Nothing strenuous. Nothing that would require you to lift more than you're comfortable with."

"I can do that," I said.

"On a scale of one to ten, how is your cooking?" He asked.

I smiled at this question. "Oh, say I'm probably a seven. Over the years I've learned to cook almost anything in a cookbook. I like to try new dishes."

"The nearest grocery store is in West Thorpton, which is an hour drive for us. We only do our shopping once every two weeks - sometimes more. Menu planning can be a challenge. Do you think you can deal with that?"

"I don't know why not." I replied. "I have, for example, a pretty good grasp of how long fresh produce lasts."

He seemed to like my response. "How about your time off? What do you like to do?"

This was probably one of the most crucial questions. "I read a lot. I've always had a special place in my heart for books. I also dabble in writing but am not very good at it. I also meditate."

His eyes lit up. "Lorraine and I are both published authors. You might enjoy our lunch conversations." Then he added, "We try to avoid 'shop talk' at the dinner table."

"You'd have your own space," James continued. "A bedroom and bathroom on the west end of the house. It's the former master bedroom upstairs-quite large. We moved to the first floor because of Lorry's wheelchair. It has a small kitchenette and its own entrance if you prefer to use them. You'd be the only one up there."

"This is a salaried job," he continued. "During the day you'd be available to do the laundry, cleaning, meals, etc. as needed. The house has five bedrooms but, there is just the two of us now, so most of the rooms are empty. Lorry and I don't generate much mess. We think you'd actually only work 2–3 hours per day. Except for mealtimes you'd, pretty much, manage your own time. Outside those hours, the time is yours. Lorraine and I maintain separate schedules. We value quiet and solitude as much as you apparently do."

"How many people are at the property?" I asked.

"Three, total," he said. "Myself, Lorraine, you. We also have a groundskeeper who lives in a separate guest house which would not be your responsibility. Michael. He's my nephew. He keeps to himself largely. You probably won't interact with him much. The property is a working tree farm on a thousand acres just a few miles from the Pacific coast which means we get lots of cool, misty, foggy, rainy weather. The house is buried deep in temperate rainforest. It has been the generational home of the Hahn family since the late 1800s."

"We call it 'The Hahnestery'. Someone once called it that because the isolation reminded him of a monastery. The family name is Hahn. Hahnestery. The name stuck."

Wow! A 'monastery' in a thousand acres of rainforest! A working 'tree farm'. Guest house. Separate spaces. A man who valued quiet. A woman in a wheelchair who probably wanted as much independence as she could have. This wasn't a trap. This was someone who'd designed a life deliberately and needed help maintaining it.

"What's the pay?" I asked.

"Twelve hundred dollars bi-weekly, plus room and board," James said. "We'll put you on the farm's payroll as one of our employees, so it will all be legal. That means you'd pay taxes."

He continued, "Breakfast is at eight; lunch is at noon; cocktails at four-thirty and dinner is at six, Sunday through Friday. Saturday is your day off - we won't expect anything from you then. You can manage your own meals on Saturdays, or you can join us at ours. After six months of probation, we'll offer health insurance."

Six hundred dollars weekly. That was nearly twenty-five hundred a month. I did the math automatically. Within a few months, I could have enough for first and last month's deposit on an apartment if I needed to leave. Within six, I could be solvent again.

"When would I start?" I asked.

"How soon could you be ready?" James asked.

"Immediately," I said. "I have everything I own with me."

He glanced at the duffel bag, and for a moment I was certain he saw through it all-the homelessness, the desperation, the latex tucked inside beneath the clothes. But his face didn't change.

"Why don't we do this next. I'll take you to the house, you can meet Lorraine, have a meal, look around and ask questions. If any of us don't think it would work out, I'll bring you back here to the library. Otherwise, you'd start work tomorrow."

"One more thing," James said. He'd shifted into a different mode-still calm, still measured, but with something underneath that suggested he was about to say something important. "We're going to ask you not to discuss our property with anyone. Not the location, not our names in context of employment, not our routines. Privacy is central to how we live. It's not punishment. It's just a boundary we maintain. We'll ask you to sign a Non-Disclosure Agreement. Are you OK with that?"

I thought about Frank. About social media and how he quickly made my secret public to all of our 'friends'. About the shelter social worker asking questions. About how the simplest thing-where you lived, what you did-could become narrative fodder.
